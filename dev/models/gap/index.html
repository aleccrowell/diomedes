Gap and pace + loss models

Outcome: % gap to the winner

y = 100·log(T / T_winner)

This is the percentage gap to the stage winner, on a log scale. Times are racing times: the official time minus any red-flag suspension (suspended_ms).

Lapped cars have no time, but their lap count bounds it. Assume constant pace, and take a car that completed l of the winner's L laps. It finished its l-th lap after the winner finished, but less than one of its own laps later. So its full-distance gap is interval-censored:

y ∈ [100·log(L/l), 100·log(L/(l−1)))

Lapped cars are identified by status ("+N Laps", "Lapped"), not by position. Retirements are dropped (the selection check below shows this barely moves the effects).

julia> g = prepare_gaps(fetch_results(ErgastCSV(FIXTURE), 2019))
GapData(17 timed + 16 lapped obs, 17 competitors, 10 machine-seasons, 2 races)

Mean structure

μ = γ[race] + c − mean(c over the race),     c = driver effect + car-season effect
  • Race intercepts γ are the expected gap of the race's average entrant. A dominant winner therefore doesn't shift the whole field.
  • Driver effects sum to zero.
  • Car-season effects sum to zero within each season. Shifting every car in a season one way and every race intercept in that season the other leaves predictions unchanged, so only within-season car differences are identified. Drivers changing teams link the seasons.
  • Priors for quantities in % are set in units of the dataset's own gap scale (PriorScale): about 1.15 for F1 and 4.9 for WRC.

Gap model: Student-t noise

gap_effects / fit_gaps: the mean above plus symmetric noise, StudentTNoise(4) (default) or NormalNoise. Heavy tails matter: with Gaussian noise, adding lapped cars inflated σ from 0.48% to 3.7%; Student-t(4) brings it to about 1.0%.

Pace + loss model

paceloss_effects / fit_paceloss split the noise into two sources:

y = μ + ε + L,    ε ~ Normal(0, σ)                       pace noise
                  L = 0 with probability 1 − π            clean race
                  L ~ Exponential(mean λ) with prob. π    incident loss (only ever slower)

Spins, damage and slow stops can only lose time, which symmetric noise can only approximate. The density is a mixture of a Gaussian and an exponentially modified Gaussian, with numerically stable closed forms for both the density and lapped cars' interval probabilities (paceloss_logpdf, paceloss_loginterval).

Identification notes

  • Age curve: only its shape up to a linear tilt is identified (the age–period–cohort problem). A linear age term is absorbed by the race centring and the driver effects. Read the curve's bends, not its overall slope or the age of its minimum.
  • Career slopes are centred across drivers. They rank trajectories against the average driver's; they don't measure whether drivers improve with age.
  • Development trends are centred within each season. A trend shared by the whole field is absorbed by the race intercepts, so a dominant car can "fade" while still winning.

Results

tab = gap_effects_table(chain, g)    # competitors and machines: mean, sd, 90% interval, rows

With pace_scale, driver effects are in average-season units, and car effects are in their own season's units. Per-row career offsets are driver_offsets; per-season incident rates come from scripts/era_paths.jl.

Retirements and selection

The pace models drop retirements. If cars that retire were running slower than their effects predict, the finishers are a selected sample. src/partial.jl and scripts/selection_check.jl test this using lap times (F1, 1996–):

  • ergast_cumulative_laps and partial_gaps build each retiree's gap at its last lap, calibrated within the race to a full-distance scale.
  • with_partial_rows adds them to the results as timed rows, and partial_mask marks them.

Result (#13): retirees were running about 0.35 points slower before they stopped, but adding them doesn't materially move the driver or car effects.
