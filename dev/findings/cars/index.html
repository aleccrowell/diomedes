Cars

Car-season effects from the best race-time model. Each is relative to the other cars in the same season: the model can't compare cars across seasons (see The question and what is identifiable). Negative is faster.

One season's field

Choose a season to see its cars with 90% intervals. Each effect is the sum of a chassis part, an engine part, a works-team part and a residual (see Chassis and engines); the tooltip splits it. 2026 has the 17 races run so far.

Dominance: the fastest car's margin

For each season, the gap between the fastest and second-fastest car (posterior means). A large margin is a dominant car. The margins are in each season's own units, so compare their shape more than their exact size across eras.

Cars with fewer than 10 results in a season are left out of this chart.

In-season development

How each car's form changed during its season, relative to the field: the change in its effect from the first round to the last. Negative means it got faster than the field did. The 20 largest trends in each direction (cars with at least 20 results):

Intervals are ±1.645 sd (about 90%). The spread of trends is about 0.5% per season (#27). Several of the largest match known histories, such as Force India's 2015 B-spec car and Renault's strong start and fade in 2011.

Circuit slopes

Which conditions suited a car, relative to its field: each car-season has a slope on three properties of the races it ran (#81):

  • average speed (layout length × laps ÷ the winner's time, averaged over the circuit's dry races; one sd is 26 km/h);
  • roughness, the road-surface score from the circuit tables (0 a racing surface, 2 public-road bumps, crowns and drains; one sd is 0.76);
  • air temperature at race time (one sd is 5 °C).

A slope is the change in the car's effect, in % of race time, at a race one standard deviation above the average. Positive means relatively slower there. Slopes measure only how a car's form changed between circuits, not its overall level: they average to zero over each car's own races, and over each season's cars. The 15 steepest in each direction, per dimension (cars with at least 20 results):

Intervals are ±1.645 sd (about 90%). Most slopes are small next to their uncertainty. The clearest are from 1996 on, when cars ran more races per season; earlier slopes shrink towards zero. The slopes typically spread by 0.14% per sd for speed, 0.11% for roughness and 0.09% for temperature.

Chassis and engines

Each car-season's effect combines four parts (#95, #100):

  • chassis: the works that built the car, followed across its seasons as a random walk. A rename keeps the works: Toleman → Benetton → Renault → Lotus → Alpine is one walk (Enstone), as is Jordan → … → Aston Martin (Silverstone).
  • engine: the works that built the engine, also a random walk. Rebadged engines go to their builder: TAG Heuer is Renault, Petronas is Ferrari.
  • works team: a shift for cars whose engine builder also owns the chassis works (Ferrari, Mercedes from 2010, Renault's own team, and so on), against customers of the same engine.
  • residual: what's left for that car-season.

A walk moves a little each season: about 0.33% of race time per year for a chassis and 0.17% for an engine, more after a change of ownership (×2.1) or a major rule change (×1.2, one multiplier for chassis and engine rules). The walks carry most of the variation between cars (the residual's spread is 0.42%). Works teams come out about 0.33% faster than their engine's customers, but the 90% interval (−0.67% to +0.02%) just reaches zero: works teams usually also have the stronger chassis, which the walks already credit. Choose a walk to see its path, relative to each season's field (negative = faster), with 90% intervals:

Red diamonds mark seasons entered across a change of ownership or a major rule change, where the walk may take a larger step. Gaps in a line are seasons the works or builder didn't race. Engine levels partly reflect their customers: Ferrari's engine sits slightly slow throughout, likely because its customers were slower teams. Read engine levels as the engine and its customers together.
