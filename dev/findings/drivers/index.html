Drivers

Driver effects from the best race-time model (see Findings for what was fitted and how to read the units). Negative is faster.

Comparing eras

Drivers from different decades are compared through chains of team-mates and shared fields, and the pace scale κ removes era-wide changes in how spread out results are. These links are real but long. Treat differences between eras as less certain than the intervals suggest, because they rest on the model's assumptions about how pace and incidents changed over time.

Fastest drivers

Posterior means (points) and 90% intervals (lines). Use the sliders to change how many drivers are shown and the minimum number of results a driver needs. Drivers with few results get wide intervals and are shrunk towards the average.

How sure is the ranking?

Intervals on effects don't directly answer "who is the fastest?". Ranking the drivers with at least 20 results in each of the 4,000 posterior draws does: the bars show how often each driver ranks first, in the top 3 and in the top 10. Even the most likely fastest driver is first in only … of the draws.

The 25 drivers most often in the top 10 are shown. All drivers with at least 20 results are in ranks.csv.

Every driver

All 550 drivers with a classified finish, by the middle of their career. Point size is the number of results. Drag to pan, scroll to zoom, and type a name to highlight it.

The vertical axis is reversed, so faster drivers are higher.

Download the data

The tables behind these charts are drivers.csv and ranks.csv (columns described on the data page).

It has one row per driver with the posterior mean, sd and 90% interval of the effect, the number of results, the first and last season, and the career slope (% per decade of age, relative to the average driver's trajectory).
