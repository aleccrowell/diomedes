Lap times

Results of the lap-time model (see The lap-time model), fitted to green-flag laps from 1996 to 2025 (Jolpica lap times, pit laps inferred from the lap times). Besides driver and car effects it has:

  • a career slope per driver (season-level age);
  • an in-season development trend per car-season;
  • separate stint-age curves for the refuelling era (1996–2009) and since 2010;
  • driver and car-season deviations from those curves (tyre management);
  • an effect of leading the race;
  • lap 1 as its own term, with the grid slot and driver and car-season start effects (#66);
  • a population age curve shared by all drivers, as in the race model (#12);
  • car-season slopes on three circuit dimensions: average lap speed, street score and race-time air temperature (#14).

It is in development (#12). Its four chains converge to a single mode (below).

What these numbers measure

The race model and the lap model are two views of the same question from different data, and neither is the reference for the other. The race model measures the time to finish a race, from one result per driver per race since 1950. That includes pit stops, strategy, safety-car periods and incidents, and it relies on its own split between pace and incident losses. The lap model measures typical green-flag pace under race conditions, plus the start (lap 1), from about 50 laps per driver per race since 1996. That includes traffic, blue flags and the tyre, fuel and engine management drivers do when they don't need to push, but not pit-stop timing, strategy or safety-car periods. So it underrates drivers who win comfortably (they manage their pace) and flatters drivers who lose most of their time in the parts of a race it leaves out. Where the two agree, the result doesn't depend on either model's blind spots. Where they disagree, either could be wrong. Its driver ranking answers a different question from the race model's: pace on clean laps, not results.

Lap model against the race model: cars

Each point is a car-season, placed by its effect in the two models. They agree closely (correlation … over … car-seasons). The lap model's spread is larger, because clean laps leave out the safety-car bunching and strategy noise that compress race time gaps.

Lap model against the race model: drivers

Drivers agree less (correlation … over the … drivers with at least 500 clean laps), and the lap model's driver effects are less spread out. Race gaps include what the lap model leaves out: pit stops, strategy, safety-car periods and the laps lost to incidents. So back-markers look faster on laps than in races, and the front-runners less dominant. Adding starts, lead laps and tyre management did not change that (#66). The race model's effects also cover whole careers from 1950, while the lap model starts in 1996: a career that began earlier enters here only at its end.

Treat the drivers of 1996–97 with care. Ukyo Katayama, for example, is among the fastest on clean laps from 1,125 laps in 1996–97, but slow in the race model. At the edge of the data, a short career, its slope and the few team-mates linking its cars are hard to separate (#12).

Drivers with at least 500 clean laps.

The shape of a career on laps

The population age curve on clean laps: how pace changes with age for the average driver, in % of lap time (negative = faster), with a 90% band. As in the race model (Careers), only its shape is identified: it has no constant or linear part, because those can't be told apart from the drivers' career slopes and the car-seasons. Read the bends, not the slope. The curve flattens through the mid-twenties, bottoms out around 27–29 and bends back up in the early thirties. Ages under 20 and over 38 have few laps.

The vertical axis is reversed, so faster is higher.

Circuit slopes

Each car-season has a slope on three circuit dimensions, centred within the season: how much faster or slower it is, relative to the field, at a circuit one standard deviation above average. The dimensions are average lap speed (sd 20 km/h), street score (sd 0.34 on the 0–1 scale; see the circuit tables) and air temperature at race time (sd 4.8 °C). Positive means relatively slower there. The spread of the slopes across car-seasons is about 0.16 % per sd for speed and street score, and 0.11 % for temperature.

Car-seasons with at least 500 laps.

Convergence

All four chains converge to one mode. The fit before this one, without the age curve and circuit slopes, split into two modes that differed in the spread of the per-race trends (τ<sub>δ</sub> 4.60 against 4.12) and in the lap-noise mixture, without changing the effects. Here τ<sub>δ</sub> = 4.58 in every chain. Three chains adapted step sizes that left them taking about four times as many leapfrog steps per draw as the fourth (tree depth 9 against 7). They were resumed from their first block at a fixed step size of 0.0272, the step size the fourth chain had tuned to (#12). The table gives the chains' scalars.
