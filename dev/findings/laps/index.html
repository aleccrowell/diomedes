Lap times

Results of the lap-time model (see The lap-time model), fitted to green-flag laps from 1996 to 2025 (Jolpica lap times, pit laps inferred from the lap times). Besides driver and car effects it has:

  • a career slope per driver (season-level age);
  • an in-season development trend per car-season;
  • separate stint-age curves for the refuelling era (1996–2009) and since 2010;
  • driver and car-season deviations from those curves (tyre management);
  • an effect of leading the race;
  • lap 1 as its own term, with the grid slot and driver and car-season start effects (#66).

It is in development (#12). Its chains settle in two modes, which differ in a few scalars but not in the driver and car results (below).

What these numbers measure

The headline results are the race model's: it measures the outcome that counts, the time to finish a race. The lap model measures something narrower: typical green-flag pace under race conditions, plus the start (lap 1). That includes traffic, blue flags and the tyre, fuel and engine management drivers do when they don't need to push, but not pit-stop timing, strategy or safety-car periods. So it underrates drivers who win comfortably (they manage their pace) and flatters drivers who lose most of their time in the parts of a race it leaves out. Read it as supporting evidence. Its car-season effects check the race model's split between car and driver, from largely independent data. Its in-season development trends are measured race by race. Don't read it as a second driver ranking.

Lap model against the race model: cars

Each point is a car-season, placed by its effect in the two models. They agree closely (correlation … over … car-seasons). The lap model's spread is larger, because clean laps leave out the safety-car bunching and strategy noise that compress race time gaps.

Lap model against the race model: drivers

Drivers agree less (correlation … over the … drivers with at least 500 clean laps), and the lap model's driver effects are less spread out. Race gaps include what the lap model leaves out: pit stops, strategy, safety-car periods and the laps lost to incidents. So back-markers look faster on laps than in races, and the front-runners less dominant. Adding starts, lead laps and tyre management did not change that (#66). The race model's effects also cover whole careers from 1950, while the lap model starts in 1996: a career that began earlier enters here only at its end.

Treat the drivers of 1996–97 with care. Ukyo Katayama, for example, is among the fastest on clean laps from 1,125 laps in 1996–97, but slow in the race model. At the edge of the data, a short career, its slope and the few team-mates linking its cars are hard to separate (#12).

Two modes

The four chains settle in two modes during warm-up and stay there. They differ in how the variation in lap times is shared out. In one mode the per-race trend over the race distance varies more from race to race. In the other, more of the variation goes into the lap-noise mixture. Both modes fit the data equally well (the same log density), so the published results pool all four chains. The driver, car, tyre-management and start effects agree across the two modes, so which mode is right doesn't change them. As with the race model's modes (Checking for multiple modes), the scalars that split the modes are not converged when the chains are pooled. Within each mode they are (R-hat ≤ 1.012), as are the cars, tyre management and starts. The driver effects and career slopes are short of the targets: R-hat 1.04 pooled over the four chains (bulk ESS 119), up to 1.07 within a mode, against 1.01 for the previous fit without the #66 terms.
