Lap times

Results of the lap-time model (see The lap-time model), fitted to green-flag laps from 2011 to the 2026 races run when it was fitted (Jolpica lap times, recorded pit stops). Besides driver and car effects it has:

  • each driver's peak pace and how often and how far their laps fall short of it (#113, below);
  • traffic around each lap (dirty air, blue flags, lapped cars, a car close behind or a big lead), and the field's pace on each lap of a race, with a slope per car-season (#113);
  • a career slope per driver (season-level age);
  • a stint-age curve, with driver and car-season deviations from it (tyre management);
  • an effect of leading the race;
  • lap 1 as its own term, with the grid slot and car-season start effects (#66);
  • a population age curve shared by all drivers, as in the race model (#12);
  • car-season slopes on three circuit dimensions: average lap speed, street score and race-time air temperature (#14);
  • from the race model (#108): walls, mixed and wet races on the incident terms, per-driver incident proneness, big incident losses, and each team's form as a walk from race to race in place of a fixed car-season effect.

It has no tyre compound or weather terms: those are known only from 2021, and a separate 2021–2026 fit with them is below.

It is in development: per-lap conditions are in progress (#116, results so far). Its four chains converge to a single mode (below). The previous fit, 1996–2025 without the #108 and #113 terms, is described in #66 and #12. Its driver effects correlate 0.88 with this fit's mean pace (39 drivers with at least 2,000 laps in both).

What these numbers measure

The race model and the lap model are two views of the same question from different data, and neither is the reference for the other. The race model measures the time to finish a race, from one result per driver per race since 1950. That includes pit stops, strategy, safety-car periods and incidents, and it relies on its own split between pace and incident losses. The lap model measures green-flag pace under race conditions, plus the start (lap 1), from about 50 laps per driver per race since 2011. Its driver effects in the comparisons below are mean pace: the peak less the laps that fall short of it. Traffic is now a term of its own, but the tyre, fuel and engine management drivers do when they don't need to push stays in, apart from what the lead and big-lead terms take. Pit-stop timing, strategy and safety-car periods are left out. So it underrates drivers who win comfortably (they manage their pace) and flatters drivers who lose most of their time in the parts of a race it leaves out. Where the two agree, the result doesn't depend on either model's blind spots. Where they disagree, either could be wrong. Its driver ranking answers a different question from the race model's: pace on clean laps, not results.

Lap model against the race model: cars

Each point is a car-season, placed by its effect in the two models. They agree closely (correlation … over … car-seasons). The lap model's spread is larger, because clean laps leave out the safety-car bunching and strategy noise that compress race time gaps.

Lap model against the race model: drivers

Drivers agree less (correlation … over the … drivers with at least 500 clean laps), and the lap model's driver effects are less spread out. Race gaps include what the lap model leaves out: pit stops, strategy, safety-car periods and the laps lost to incidents. So back-markers look faster on laps than in races, and the front-runners less dominant. Adding starts, lead laps and tyre management did not change that (#66). The pattern holds in this fit, with traffic as its own term: lap-model mean pace rises about 0.45 % per 1 % of race-model effect. The race model's effects also cover whole careers from 1950, while this fit starts in 2011: a career that began earlier (Alonso, Räikkönen, Massa, Button, Webber) enters here only from 2011.

Drivers with at least 500 clean laps.

Peak pace and consistency

The model splits each driver's laps in two (#113). The peak is their clean-lap pace on the car's limit. On other laps they fall short of it. How often, and by how much, varies by driver and by car-season, because a hard car to drive shouldn't read as inconsistent drivers. Mean pace is the peak plus the expected shortfall (how often × how far). All three are in % of lap time against the drivers' average, lower = better. The shortfall is net of the traffic, lead and big-lead terms, of the field's pace on the lap, and of incidents, which have their own losses.

Most of the difference between drivers is peak pace. Over the … drivers with at least 2,000 laps, the peaks spread about six times as much as the expected shortfalls (sd 0.22 % against 0.04 %), and they are unrelated (correlation …). Peak and mean pace correlate …. Max Verstappen is the outlier on both: the fastest peak (−0.66 %) and the smallest expected shortfall (0.075 %), because he falls short on 8 % of laps, where the other drivers do on 12–37 %. Drivers differ mostly in how often they fall short, not how far: the mean size of a shortfall stays between 0.7 % and 1.1 %. Car-seasons vary more than drivers in both.

A caveat: a shortfall the driver chose and one they couldn't avoid look the same. Beyond the traffic and lead terms, a car managing its pace (holding a position, saving tyres or fuel) reads as falling short. And this fit has no tyre or weather terms, so wet and mixed laps go mostly to the shortfall rather than to a slower limit, and drivers with more wet laps may look less consistent than they are. The 2021–2026 fit with those terms is below (#116).

Drivers with at least 2,000 clean laps, at the base rates: a dry race, with a car whose own shortfall offsets are zero.

Track conditions (2021–2026)

From 2021 the F1 timing data says which tyre compound each lap was run on, the track temperature and whether rain was falling (#116). A second fit adds these to the model above, over 2021–2026 only. It has every term of the main fit, plus:

  • an offset for each compound against the SOFT, and a stint slope per compound on top of the shared stint curve;
  • a slope on track temperature, against the race's mean;
  • rain on the peak, and wet tyres and rain on how often and how far laps fall short;
  • each driver's deviation on wet-tyre laps (intermediates and wets) from their dry pace.

Its conditions terms and wet deviations converge (R-hat ≤ 1.015, bulk ESS ≥ 400). Its driver and car-season levels don't yet (R-hat up to 1.09). They mix slowly along the ridge between a dominant driver and their car: Verstappen against Red Bull in 2023, Norris and Piastri against McLaren in 2025. So the rankings on this page stay on the 2011–2026 fit, and this section shows only the conditions results.

Compounds. Against the SOFT, a MEDIUM lap is 0.16 % slower and a HARD lap 0.24 % slower, at the same stint age. Both lose less pace through a stint than the SOFT, by 0.06 % and 0.10 % per 10 laps. An INTERMEDIATE lap is 7.3 % slower than a dry SOFT lap and a WET lap 14.2 % slower. On those tyres laps get faster through the stint (−0.9 % and −3.5 % per 10 laps). That is probably mostly the track drying or the rain easing, not the tyres.

Temperature and rain. A track 10 °C hotter than the race's mean makes the lap 0.26 % slower. Rain falling doesn't slow the peak beyond the tyres (−0.20 %); it goes to the shortfall instead. In rain, laps fall short of the peak about 7.5 times as often (logit +2.0) and by 1.5 times as much. On wet tyres laps fall short by less (×0.44), as the tyres' own offsets already carry the slower limit, and how often they fall short is barely identified (logit +1.3 ± 0.7).

The field's pace. The slope of a lap on the field's pace that lap stays at 0.13 with these terms in. Conditions, then, don't explain why the field pace moves a driver's limit only weakly. The main fit's slope (0.17) is over 2011–2026, so the two aren't like for like.

Wet-weather skill

The wet deviations are centred across drivers. A negative one means the driver loses less pace on wet tyres than the others do, relative to their own dry pace. They spread with an sd of 0.73 % (± 0.17). Over the … drivers with at least 100 wet laps, they run from about −0.66 % (Magnussen, Norris, Vettel, Verstappen) to +0.85 % (Albon). 15 of the 21 differ from zero by more than 1.645 sd.

Read them with three caveats:

  • They include the car. Car-seasons' own wet deviations couldn't be identified: in both probes, 2023 cars with 30–50 wet laps, all from two mixed races, came out several % faster or slower in the wet. So a driver's deviation also carries how well their car worked in the wet. Norris and Piastri share a McLaren.
  • Few races. Wet-tyre laps are 4.7 % of the laps, from 18 races, so one race goes a long way.
  • Wet tyres, not rain. The deviation is on intermediate and wet-tyre laps. A damp track on slicks counts as dry.

Drivers with at least 100 wet-tyre laps, 2021–2026: posterior mean and ±1.645 sd (about a 90 % interval).

The shape of a career on laps

The population age curve on clean laps: how pace changes with age for the average driver, in % of lap time (negative = faster), with a 90% band. As in the race model (Careers), only its shape is identified: it has no constant or linear part, because those can't be told apart from the drivers' career slopes and the car-seasons. Read the bends, not the slope. Pace improves steeply through the late teens, by about 0.40 % from 17 to 21, then stays within about ±0.06 % from 21 to 33. From 34 on it jumps from one year to the next: each age there rests on a handful of drivers (Alonso, Räikkönen, Hamilton, Button), so it mostly follows their seasons, and it shows no clear decline.

The vertical axis is reversed, so faster is higher.

Circuit slopes

Each car-season has a slope on three circuit dimensions, centred within the season: how much faster or slower it is, relative to the field, at a circuit one standard deviation above average. The dimensions are average lap speed (sd 20 km/h), street score (sd 0.35 on the 0–1 scale; see the circuit tables) and air temperature at race time (sd 4.8 °C). Positive means relatively slower there. The spread of the slopes across car-seasons is about 0.11 % per sd for speed, 0.08 % for street score and 0.05 % for temperature. That's smaller than in the 1996–2025 fit (0.16, 0.16 and 0.11 %). This fit covers fewer seasons, and its race-to-race walk of each team's form can take up some of what a fixed car-season effect left to the circuit slopes; which of the two matters more hasn't been checked.

Car-seasons with at least 500 laps.

Convergence

All four chains converge to one mode: τ<sub>δ</sub> = 4.03 and lap noise σ = 0.521 % in every chain, with no divergences. They sampled at the same tree depth (about 7.6, 220–233 leapfrog steps per draw) and the same step size (0.038–0.039). The driver, car-season, peak and race-walk levels mix most slowly (bulk ESS 310–380 from 4,000 draws). The shortfall, tyre, start, circuit and field-pace terms all have an ESS above 1,000. The first full fit with these terms diverged on nearly every iteration, because the field pace moved the clean-lap edge through the shortfall. With the edge set by the base shortfall only, it didn't diverge at all (#113). The table gives the chains' scalars.
