Model progression

Each term was added in its own issue, fitted to every classified F1 finish (1950–2019, 4 chains × 1,000 draws) and kept only if it improved out-of-sample fit. Fit is measured as expected log predictive density (elpd) by PSIS-LOO; gains are over the previous row unless stated. The first two rows were fitted before the red-flag correction (#19).

What each term found

Race duration (#19). Longer races don't have more incidents (slope on logit π −0.014 ± 0.073), but each incident costs a smaller share of a long race (slope on log λ −0.071 ± 0.025).

Pace scale κ (#20). A season's spread of results is not constant: κ is about 2.5 in the early 1950s, about 1.4 in the mid-1960s and the mid-1980s turbo era, and 0.55–0.6 in 2013–19. The largest steps fall in 1989 and 2010. Without κ, drivers of the 1950s, when gaps were large, absorb the spread: the best driver's effect shrank from −2.58% to −2.08% when κ was added.

Age curve (#22). Concave: drivers are slowest when young (+0.54 at 18), reach a plateau around 25–33 (about −0.1), and slow again from about 36 (+0.34 at 40). Only the curvature is identified (see The question and what is identifiable).

Career slopes (#24). Beyond the shared curve, drivers' trajectories differ by about 1.08% per decade of age (τ). Some examples: Kubica declined (+1.4, his comeback after injury) and Verstappen improved fast (−1.4).

In-season development (#27). Cars' form relative to the field changes during a season by about 0.5% per season (τ_dev = 0.50 ± 0.05). The largest trends match known histories: Force India's 2015 B-spec car (−0.62 ± 0.27), Renault 2011's strong start and fade (+0.97 ± 0.30). Trends are relative to the field, so a dominant car can "fade" while still winning (McLaren 1988, +0.59).

Circuit slopes, walls and rain (#81). Each car-season has a slope on its races' average speed, roughness (the road surface score) and air temperature. Across car-seasons the slopes spread by about 0.15% of race time per standard deviation of speed (26 km/h), 0.11% for roughness and 0.09% for temperature (5 °C). McLaren 2024 has the field's steepest temperature slope (faster in hot races). Rain dominates incidents: the odds of a routine loss are about 3.4× higher in a mixed race and 10× in a wet one, and each loss is 16–27% larger. Walls hardly change how often losses happen but make them about 23% larger. Car and driver effects barely move (correlation 0.996 with the previous fit).

Chassis and engine walks (#95). Each car-season's effect combines the works that built the chassis and the works that built the engine, each a random walk over its seasons, plus a residual. A rename keeps a works (Toro Rosso → AlphaTauri); rebadged engines go to their builder (TAG Heuer = Renault). The chassis walk moves about 0.35% of race time per year, the engine walk about 0.17%; a new works starts about 1.0% from the field. Steps are larger at a change of ownership (×1.9) and at major chassis or engine rule changes (×1.2 and ×1.4; the two can't be told apart). The walks carry about 84% of the car-season variance: the residual spread is 0.43%, against 1.06% for the car-season effects before. The gain is modest and comes from clean dry races, mostly in the 1980s and 2010s. It doesn't make the sparse 1950s car-seasons much more precise (posterior sd −10%). Linking a team's seasons moves some pace from drivers to cars: driver effects correlate 0.97 with the previous fit, but their spread shrinks from 0.74% to 0.59%. The largest shifts are drivers who raced only for weak teams (Bianchi and Chilton at Marussia move about 1.3–1.5% faster), and Verstappen is now the most likely fastest driver.

Works team, one rule multiplier and driver incident proneness (#97, #99–#102). Three changes and the 2026 races, fitted together:

  • one multiplier for chassis and engine rule changes (×1.22 per step). The walk fit's two could not be told apart.
  • a works-team term: cars whose engine builder owns the chassis works come out 0.33% faster [−0.67, 0.02] than customers of the same engine.
  • per-driver incident proneness: an offset on each driver's log-odds of an incident, with spread 1.33 [1.13, 1.57]. This is the largest of the three, and is behind most of the gain (see Speed and incident proneness).

The gain is +79 ± 19 on the 16,047 shared rows where PSIS-LOO is reliable, and +90 ± 22 on all shared rows. Most of it comes from the 2010s (+37 ± 8). Car-season effects barely move (correlation 0.996). Speed work cut the fit time from ~16.4 h to ~10.4 h per chain at the same tree depth, without changing the posterior (#102): vectorised priors, no per-call copies of constant data, and closed-form mixture derivatives in the likelihood.

How models were compared

  • PSIS-LOO (Pareto-smoothed importance-sampling leave-one-out) on every row: timed and lapped results alike, so all models are scored on the same data (pointwise_loglik).
  • Its weak spot. Rows with Pareto k > 0.7 make the estimate unreliable. There are 350 such rows (2.7%) for the era models, rising to 814 for the development model, 869 with the circuit terms, 792 with the walks and 695 with incident proneness (#18, #27, #81, #95). They are well-fitted rows that alone carry much of a sparse parameter, mostly 1950s–60s car-seasons with a few races (#91). From #95 on, comparisons also report the gain on rows with k ≤ 0.7 in both fits (scripts/pareto_k_summary.jl).
  • Grouped K-fold checks the close calls. Each race's results are spread over 5 folds, so every race keeps an intercept. K-fold confirmed the era ranking: random walk over decade by 43.5 ± 15.2, and over regime by 54.6 ± 16.9 (#35). It hasn't been run on the larger models, where one fold takes 3.6–4.6 hours on the Raspberry Pi 5 used for these fits (#18).
