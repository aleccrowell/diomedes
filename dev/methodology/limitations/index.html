Limitations

  • Comparing eras rests on long chains of links. Driver effects are comparable across decades only through team-mates and shared fields, and through the model's assumptions about how pace spread (κ) and incidents changed. Even with κ, the top of the all-time driver list is dominated by drivers from before 1980 (#20).
  • Car effects are within-season only. They can't say whether the 2004 Ferrari was faster than the 1988 McLaren, only how far each was ahead of its own field.
  • Separating a driver from their car relies on team-mates and team changes. It is the main remaining source of slow mixing (#20), and it is weakest for drivers who spent their careers with one team.
  • The age curve's peak isn't identified, only its shape; career slopes are relative to the average driver (#22, #24).
  • Pace noise against routine losses (σ against π, λ) is weakly identified (#32). The effects don't depend on it, but those parameters shouldn't be read as precise.
  • Model comparison is getting harder. As the models grow, more rows have Pareto k > 0.7, which makes PSIS-LOO less reliable. Grouped K-fold has confirmed the era-term ranking, but hasn't been run on the latest terms (#18).
  • In-season development is linear, one trend per car-season. A random walk over rounds, and car × circuit interactions, are not modelled (#14).
  • Tyre compounds aren't in the data. The lap model can't separate compound choice (for example, the 2014–21 rule that the top 10 qualifiers start on their Q2 tyres) from pace (#12).
  • Rallying lacks covariates. The WRC fit has no road order or surface terms, and its driver effects are relative to a 641-driver field that is mostly amateurs (#33, #28).
