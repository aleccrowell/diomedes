The lap-time model

The race-time models get one number per driver per race. Lap times (Jolpica, 1996 on) give about 50 per driver per race, including the laps a car completed before retiring. The lap-time model (#12) fits

\[y = 100\log\frac{t}{\text{median clean lap}} = \gamma_r + \delta_r\,(f - \bar f_r) + a_d + b_m + \beta_1 s + \beta_2 s^2 - (\text{race mean}) + \varepsilon + L ,\]

with $f$ the lap's fraction of the race distance (fuel burn, track evolution), $s$ the tyres' stint age, and the same pace + loss noise as the race model. The current fit (2011–2026, recorded pit stops; #108, #113) adds:

  • peak pace and shortfall (#113): the small-loss part of the noise becomes each driver's shortfall from their peak. Its probability and size get driver and car-season offsets, so a driver has a peak (the clean-lap edge), a frequency and a size of falling short, and a mean pace (peak plus expected shortfall). The driver and car effects are sampled on the mean pace, which the data pins tightly, and the peak is derived from it;
  • traffic: shared effects of the gap to the car ahead on track (dirty air), of lapped cars ahead and blue flags, of a car close behind on the same lap (defending), and of leading by over 5 s (cruising), from cumulative lap times;
  • conditions, in a separate 2021–2026 fit only (#116): the tyre compound, track temperature and rain on each lap, and drivers' deviations on wet tyres;
  • field pace: the field's median lap, smoothed over neighbouring laps, beyond the race's mean and trend, with a slope per car-season, for conditions that change within a race (a drying track, rain arriving). It also moves the shortfall's probability and size, but not the edge;
  • from the race model (#108): walls, mixed and wet races on the incident probability and size; per-driver incident proneness; a share of incidents that are big losses; and a walk of each team's chassis form from race to race, which replaces the fixed car-season effect and the in-season development trend;
  • a career slope per driver (on season-level age), as in the race model;
  • a stint-age curve, with driver and car-season deviations from it (tyre management), on stint age centred per driver and per car-season;
  • an effect of leading the race on the lap (clean air against managing a lead; the two can't be told apart);
  • lap 1 as its own term: its time against the race's median lap 1, with the drivers' and cars' pace effects, a curve in the grid slot, and car-season start effects, with its own noise. Per-driver start effects are off, because they were barely identified;
  • the race model's population age curve, shared by all drivers (#12);
  • car-season slopes on three circuit dimensions, centred within each season: average lap speed, street score and race-time air temperature (#14). The covariates come from hand-checked circuit tables, with sources, in reference/circuits/.

It is fitted to green-flag laps. Excluded:

  • lap 1 from the pace term (it enters the start term);
  • safety-car, VSC and red-flag laps, detected as race-wide slow laps;
  • pit in- and out-laps (recorded).

No lap is dropped for its own size: a per-car cut on slow laps, like inferred pit stops, would also drop a car's largest incidents (about 5 per 1,000 laps, more for incident-prone drivers; #108). Traffic, blue flags, mistakes and damage stay in: the traffic terms, the shortfall and the incident losses, big ones included, take them.

What it measures

Green-flag pace under race conditions, plus the start, not race results. Since #113 it separates a driver's peak from how often and how far they fall short of it. A driver well ahead lifts and coasts and saves tyres, so the lap model underrates comfortable winners. Pit-stop timing, strategy and safety-car periods are left out. So its driver effects are less spread out than the race model's, compressed most at the front. Neither model is the reference for the other. The race model measures results, from one observation per driver per race. The lap model measures clean-lap pace, from about 50 times more observations, which constrains the split between car and driver and in-season development more tightly within its seasons. Where the two agree, the result holds across both models' blind spots. Where they disagree, either could be wrong: independent checks, such as held-out races or simulated data with known effects, are needed to tell which. Adding starts, lead laps and tyre management did not reduce the compression: the slope of lap-model driver effects on race-model ones stayed about 0.41 over 1996–2025, while the correlation rose slightly, from 0.67 to 0.69 (#66).

Status

This model is in development. The published fit has the #108 and #113 terms; per-lap conditions are in progress in #116.

  • Pit laps can be inferred from lap times. Over 2011–2019, excluding inferred pit laps instead of Ergast's recorded ones gives the same effects: driver correlation 0.998, car-season correlation 1.000, no difference larger than its posterior uncertainty. That opens up 1996–2010, before pit data.
  • It agrees with the race model on cars, less on drivers. Over 2011–19, the correlation with the race model is 0.97 for car-seasons and 0.70 for drivers. The race model's driver effects span whole careers, with age and career-slope terms; the lap model's cover only those seasons.
  • The tyre term doesn't move the effects. Removing the stint-age terms changes the driver and car effects by less than their posterior uncertainty. Tyre wear is real variance within a race, but almost unrelated to who is driving.
  • 1996–2025, with career slopes and development, no tyre terms. Cars agree with the race model at 0.96 over 320 car-seasons, drivers at 0.68–0.70. The drivers of 1996–97, at the edge of the data, are the least reliable.
  • 1996–2025 with the #66 terms. Its drivers correlate 0.977 with the fit without them. Laps get faster through a stint in the refuelling era (−0.31 % per 10 laps, as fuel burns off) and slower since 2010 (+0.60 %). The chains settle in two modes of equal log density. The modes differ in the spread of the per-race trends and in the lap-noise mixture. The driver, car, tyre-management and start effects agree across them (correlation ≥ 0.998).
  • 1996–2025 with the age curve and circuit slopes. One mode; max R-hat 1.014, no divergences. Car-seasons correlate 0.953 with the race model (0.954 before), drivers 0.761 (0.718 before). Its effects correlate 0.989 (cars) and 0.954 (drivers) with the previous fit. The circuit slopes vary by about 0.16 % per sd of speed and of street score, and 0.11 % for temperature.
  • 2011–2026 with the #108 and #113 terms (the current fit). One mode, max R-hat 1.018, no divergences, 4 × 1,000 draws. Car-seasons correlate 0.97 with the race model, drivers' mean pace 0.66 (drivers with at least 500 laps). Peak pace carries most of the driver spread: across drivers with at least 2,000 laps the peaks vary about six times as much as the expected shortfalls, and the two are unrelated. The field pace moves the edge only weakly (slope 0.17, where 1 would mean conditions slow the limit fully), and wet and mixed laps go mostly to the shortfall (#116).
  • 2021–2026 with per-lap conditions (#116). Tyre compound, track temperature and rain from the F1 timing data, and drivers' deviations on wet tyres. INTERMEDIATE laps are 7.3 % and WET laps 14.2 % slower than SOFT ones. Rain goes to the shortfall rather than the peak. Drivers' wet deviations spread with an sd of 0.73 %, but carry their car's wet performance: the cars' own couldn't be identified from so few wet races. The field-pace slope stays at 0.13 with these terms in. The conditions terms converge; the driver and car levels don't yet (R-hat 1.08–1.09), so the rankings stay on the 2011–2026 fit (results).
