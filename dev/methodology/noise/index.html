Noise: pace and incidents

Two sources of variation

Most of a race's spread is small, symmetric variation in pace. Some of it is something else: a spin, a slow stop, damage, a penalty. That can only lose time, and sometimes a great deal of it. The pace + loss model writes the residual of each result as

\[y = \mu + \varepsilon + L, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma), \qquad L = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{with probability } 1-\pi \\ \text{Exponential(mean } \lambda) & \text{with probability } \pi \end{cases}\]

so the density is a mixture of a Gaussian and an exponentially modified Gaussian (EMG). The figure draws it from that formula; move the sliders to see how the two components shape the slow tail.

Both the density and the interval probabilities of lapped cars have closed forms. These need care in the tails, where naive formulas cancel catastrophically (paceloss_effects).

The alternative, symmetric heavy-tailed noise, is much worse. With Gaussian noise, adding lapped cars inflated σ from 0.48% to 3.7%, and Student-t(4) brought it to about 1.0%. Even so, Student-t(4) noise is about 1,660 elpd behind the plain pace + loss model, and 2,905 ± 110 behind pace + loss with era terms (pl_rw) (#19; fits from before the red-flag correction).

Incidents vary by race and era

The incident probability $\pi$ and the mean loss $\lambda$ vary by race:

\[\operatorname{logit}\pi_r = a_\pi + \beta^\pi_\text{dur}\, d_r + \text{era}^\pi_{s(r)}, \qquad \log\lambda_r = a_\lambda + \beta^\lambda_\text{dur}\, d_r + \text{era}^\lambda_{s(r)} ,\]

with $d_r$ the race's standardised log duration. The era terms are random walks over seasons with Student-t(3) steps: mostly smooth, with occasional jumps. That beat effects for each decade (by 53 ± 15 elpd) and for a priori regulation eras (68 ± 17) (#19). Grouped K-fold cross-validation confirmed the ranking (#35).

A second, bigger loss

A single result can dominate a season's λ. Malaysia 2011's car 42 laps down pushed that season's mean loss to 10.7% (#16). So losses get two components:

\[L \sim (1-\rho)\,\text{Exponential}(\lambda_r) + \rho\,\text{Exponential}(\lambda_2), \qquad \lambda_2 = \lambda_\text{base}\, e^{\delta}, \ \delta > 0 .\]

With it, about 2% of incidents are big losses (ρ, with $\lambda_2$ about 6.2× the baseline, roughly 10%). The routine component fires about 73% of the time with a mean of 1.7%, and σ falls from 0.51 to 0.40 (#23). The fit improved by 155 ± 23 elpd (#23).

Reading π and λ

With the big-loss component, the "incident" component mostly describes routine time lost in a race (traffic, a slow stop), not crashes. π and λ are better read as the shape of the slow tail than as literal incident rates (#23).

The race level and the pace/loss split

The split between pace noise σ and routine losses (π, λ) is only weakly identified. Shifting every race intercept trades against it at almost no likelihood cost. With a fixed prior on the intercepts, the summed prior over about 1,000 races decided the split, and changing that prior moved σ from 0.36 to 0.40 (#32). Learning the common race level $\mu_\gamma$ removes that pull at equal out-of-sample fit (2.9 ± 6.0 elpd), and is the default (#36). The remaining uncertainty is honest: the incident intercept $a_\pi$ has posterior sd 0.19 (#32). The driver and car effects don't depend on the split.

Two modes in the loss mixture

The Jolpica refit of 1950–2025 (#55) adds the 2020–2025 seasons, lapped cars at exact gaps (from 2023, when their own times are recorded), and retirements as partial gaps (from 1996). Its chains didn't all settle in the same place:

(The exported fit has a single mode.)

Both modes describe the same slow tail of results, split differently between the two loss components. Each row below is one chain (posterior mean and 90% interval):

In mode A, of incidents are big losses and the routine loss averages %. In mode B, are big and the routine loss averages %: fewer big losses and larger routine ones, with slightly less pace noise.

Mode B is the posterior; A is a local mode. B's mean log density is about 150 higher, its log-likelihood about 145 higher, and it predicts better out of sample (PSIS-LOO, 130 ± 25 elpd; #55). A difference that size means A holds a negligible share of the posterior: chains that start near it stay there, but the correct posterior summary is B alone. So the tables on these pages use B's chains, and the export drops any mode whose mean log density is more than 10 below the best one's (#64). Mode A is still shown, here and dashed in Eras.

What depends on the mode. The driver and car effects barely do (the list above), so this choice doesn't move the rankings. The loss scalars, the season paths of π and λ, and the big-loss share and size do.

Why it can happen. A retirement row is a car that was running slower than its effects predict before it stopped (Do the dropped retirements bias the pace effects?). These rows add a block of moderately slow results, and either loss component can absorb it: a few more big losses, or a wider routine one. A refit without those rows will show whether they cause the second mode (#55). Half the chains landing in a local mode also means a fit like this needs more than one chain per mode to be trusted: R-hat over all chains is what flagged it.
