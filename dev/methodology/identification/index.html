The question and what is identifiable

Crossed effects

A result depends on the driver, the car and the day. With one result per driver per race, the race-time models write the expected result of driver $d$ in car-season $m$ at race $r$ as

\[\mu_{i} = \gamma_r + \kappa_{s(r)}\, a_d + b_m - \overline{\bigl(\kappa_{s(r)} a + b\bigr)}_r ,\]

where $\gamma_r$ is the race's level, $a_d$ the driver effect, $b_m$ the car-season effect, $\kappa_s$ a per-season pace scale, and the last term is the mean of the driver and car parts over the race's entrants. Driver effects are in average-season units (κ = 1); car effects, which never leave their season, are in that season's units. Driver and car effects are crossed, not nested: a driver drives several cars over a career and each car has two (or more) drivers. Team-mates separate a driver from their car, and moves between teams link cars.

What the data can and can't tell apart

Each constraint below removes a direction the likelihood can't see. Without them the posterior has ridges that the sampler wanders along, and the raw effects are meaningless even when predictions are fine.

  • Drivers sum to zero. Adding $c$ to every driver and subtracting it from every car leaves every prediction unchanged. Driver effects are therefore relative to the average driver in the data.
  • Cars sum to zero within each season. With race intercepts, shifting all of one season's cars by $c$ and all of that season's races by $-c$ changes nothing. Only differences between cars in the same season are identified, and car effects can't be compared across seasons. Drivers can: a driver who raced in 1985 and 1995 links the two seasons.
  • Race intercepts are field-centred, so $\gamma_r$ is the expected gap of the race's average entrant, not of an arbitrary reference car.
  • Age, period and cohort. A driver's age is the year minus their birth year. A linear age effect $\beta\cdot\text{age} = \beta\cdot\text{year} - \beta\cdot\text{birth year}$ is absorbed by the race intercepts (year) and the driver effects (birth year). Only the curvature of a population age curve is identified: whether drivers peak and decline, not at what age they peak (#22). For the same reason, per-driver career slopes are centred across drivers. They show how a driver's trajectory differed from the average driver's, not whether drivers improve with age (#24).
  • In-season development trends are centred within each season. An improvement shared by the whole field is absorbed by the race intercepts (#27).

Measuring convergence on what is identified

The constraints leave directions that only the prior sees, such as the mean of the standardised driver effects. Those mix slowly and make the raw R-hat and ESS look alarming even when every identified quantity has converged. In a WRC trial every standardised driver coordinate had ESS ≈ 21 and R-hat 1.26, while the centred effects agreed across chains to about 0.02% (#25).

Sampling directly in sum-to-zero (Helmert) coordinates left the posterior unchanged (driver correlation 0.99987) but made sampling worse: 698 divergences against 73, and min ESS 17 against 21 (#25). Instead, convergence is reported on the identified quantities (identified_convergence): centred effects, per-race incident rates and pace scales, and scalars (#26).

Centred and non-centred parametrisation

Most F1 drivers and every car-season have many results, so their effects are well determined by the data. Non-centred effects $a = \sigma z$ then tie $\sigma$ to every $z$, creating a funnel.

  • Cars: with the pace scale, non-centred cars had a ridge between $\kappa$ and the car spread (correlation down to −0.89) and needed 309 leapfrog steps per draw. Centred cars removed the ridge and cut the steps by 38% (#20).
  • Drivers: in WRC, non-centred drivers gave $\sigma_\text{comp}$ R-hat 1.13 and ESS 37. Centring raised the scalars' min ESS from 106 to 1,168 in a trial (#28). One outlier driver, about 29% off the pace on almost every stage, stayed bimodal until drivers got a Student-t(3) prior (#28).
