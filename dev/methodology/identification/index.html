The question and what is identifiable

Crossed effects

A result depends on the driver, the car and the day. With one result per driver per race, the race-time models write the expected result of driver $d$ in car-season $m$ at race $r$ as

\[\mu_{i} = \gamma_r + \kappa_{s(r)}\, a_d + b_m - \overline{\bigl(\kappa_{s(r)} a + b\bigr)}_r ,\]

where $\gamma_r$ is the race's level, $a_d$ the driver effect, $b_m$ the car-season effect, $\kappa_s$ a per-season pace scale, and the last term is the mean of the driver and car parts over the race's entrants. Driver effects are in average-season units (κ = 1); car effects, which never leave their season, are in that season's units. Driver and car effects are crossed, not nested: a driver drives several cars over a career and each car has two (or more) drivers. Team-mates separate a driver from their car, and moves between teams link cars.

What the data can and can't tell apart

Each constraint below removes a direction the likelihood can't see. Without them the posterior has ridges that the sampler wanders along, and the raw effects are meaningless even when predictions are fine.

  • Drivers sum to zero. Adding $c$ to every driver and subtracting it from every car leaves every prediction unchanged. Driver effects are therefore relative to the average driver in the data.
  • Cars sum to zero within each season. With race intercepts, shifting all of one season's cars by $c$ and all of that season's races by $-c$ changes nothing. Only differences between cars in the same season are identified, and car effects can't be compared across seasons. Drivers can: a driver who raced in 1985 and 1995 links the two seasons.
  • Race intercepts are field-centred, so $\gamma_r$ is the expected gap of the race's average entrant, not of an arbitrary reference car.
  • Age, period and cohort. A driver's age is the year minus their birth year. A linear age effect $\beta\cdot\text{age} = \beta\cdot\text{year} - \beta\cdot\text{birth year}$ is absorbed by the race intercepts (year) and the driver effects (birth year). Only the curvature of a population age curve is identified: whether drivers peak and decline, not at what age they peak (#22). For the same reason, per-driver career slopes are centred across drivers. They show how a driver's trajectory differed from the average driver's, not whether drivers improve with age (#24).
  • In-season development trends are centred within each season. An improvement shared by the whole field is absorbed by the race intercepts (#27).
  • Circuit slopes are centred the same way, and each car-season's circuit covariates are also centred over its own races. A slope then only describes how a car's form changed between circuits, never its overall level, and a car that raced one kind of circuit has no slope at all (#81).
  • Chassis and engine walks are centred within each season, like the car effects. Chassis and engine separate only where they cross: customer engines, a works changing engine supplier, and 1960s chassis that ran several engines. Where an engine had one user for a stretch, the split rests on the walks' priors. Engine levels mix in their customers' pace: Ferrari's engine walk sits slightly slow throughout, likely because its customers were slow teams (#95).
  • The works-team term is identified by works teams against customers of the same engine in the same seasons. Where a works team also had the best chassis, the walks and the term share the credit, which is one reason its interval is wide (#100).
  • Driver incident proneness is centred across drivers, so the season and track terms keep describing the average driver. It's identified from which drivers' results fall in the loss tail. A driver with few results shrinks towards the average (#101).

Measuring convergence on what is identified

The constraints leave directions that only the prior sees, such as the mean of the standardised driver effects. Those mix slowly and make the raw R-hat and ESS look alarming even when every identified quantity has converged. In a WRC trial every standardised driver coordinate had ESS ≈ 21 and R-hat 1.26, while the centred effects agreed across chains to about 0.02% (#25).

Sampling directly in sum-to-zero (Helmert) coordinates left the posterior unchanged (driver correlation 0.99987) but made sampling worse: 698 divergences against 73, and min ESS 17 against 21 (#25). Instead, convergence is reported on the identified quantities (identified_convergence): centred effects, per-race incident rates and pace scales, and scalars (#26).

Centred and non-centred parametrisation

Most F1 drivers and every car-season have many results, so their effects are well determined by the data. Non-centred effects $a = \sigma z$ then tie $\sigma$ to every $z$, creating a funnel.

  • Cars: with the pace scale, non-centred cars had a ridge between $\kappa$ and the car spread (correlation down to −0.89) and needed 309 leapfrog steps per draw. Centred cars removed the ridge and cut the steps by 38% (#20).
  • Drivers: in WRC, non-centred drivers gave $\sigma_\text{comp}$ R-hat 1.13 and ESS 37. Centring raised the scalars' min ESS from 106 to 1,168 in a trial (#28). One outlier driver, about 29% off the pace on almost every stage, stayed bimodal until drivers got a Student-t(3) prior (#28).

Ridges between a team's drivers and its car

Centring removes the directions no data can see, but some directions are seen only weakly. In the lap model over 2021–2026, the slowest directions in the posterior were driver–team clusters: Norris and Piastri against McLaren's race walk, Verstappen and Pérez against Red Bull's. The data fix each car's form plus its drivers' levels, and the split rests on few links (team moves, team-mates).

To find such directions, take each chain's posterior means, standardised by each quantity's posterior sd, and run an SVD of their differences between chains. The leading vectors name the quantities the chains disagree on together. For McLaren's cluster it held 65 % of the between-chain spread (#116).

The fix was to sample along the ridge rather than across it. rwrel samples each walk state relative to its drivers, $\tilde x = x + W a$, with $W$ each driver's share of the state's laps. This shear has Jacobian 1, so the model is unchanged. Moving a team's drivers no longer drags its states with them, and the minimum ESS of the drivers rose from 81 to 219 in the full fit. Adding the career slopes to the shear (rwrels) moved the slow direction onto the age curve instead, so it isn't used.

Effects that rest on a few races

Effects measured on a small part of the data, such as wet-weather skill (about 5 % of laps, from 16 races), can go wrong in three ways the well-measured effects don't (#116):

  • They absorb what the races have in common. Wet races differ: the lap model's per-race wet offsets spread with an sd of 2.4 % of lap time. Without them, a car-season with wet laps from one or two races took those races' severity: Williams 2023 came out at +2.6 % to +3.2 % against about −0.2 % in a raw same-lap comparison. Give such an effect a per-race term, partially pooled, so units are compared within a race.
  • Losses absorb weak signals. In a pace + loss model a slower lap can be read as a slower limit or as a loss. A likelihood profile along a car's wet effect showed that with about 30 wet laps the losses take up a faster wet limit and the likelihood is nearly flat. Before trusting such an effect, profile the likelihood along it, and check that its posterior widens as its data shrink.
  • Centring a subset forces counterweights. If only some units have the effect, centring within season makes the rest of the season's units balance one outlier. Centring over all units instead lets anything shared, such as a race's severity, load onto them. Choose the centring together with the terms that take up the shared part, and test it: the wetallg test checks that shifting every unit by a constant leaves the likelihood unchanged.

The lap model's wet-skill terms follow all three: per-race wet offsets (wetrace), car-season effects for every car-season shrunk by a shared spread and centred globally (wetallg), and a check that few-lap effects stay near zero with wide intervals.
